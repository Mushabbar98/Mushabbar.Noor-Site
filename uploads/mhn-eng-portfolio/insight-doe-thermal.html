What a Response-Surface DoE Buys You in Flight-Envelope Thermal Analysis

Qualifying an avionics LRU's thermal design under MIL-E-5400 means showing it stays within limits across the aircraft's full operating envelope — every altitude band, Mach number, duty cycle, and bleed-air setting the platform can fly. Taken literally, that's dozens to hundreds of distinct operating points, and running a full CFD case for each one is rarely realistic on a qualification timeline.

The sweep-everything approach breaks down fast

A brute-force sweep does two things badly. First, it's slow — each CFD case can take hours to set up, solve and post-process, and a genuinely fine-grained sweep of altitude × Mach × duty cycle × bleed setting multiplies that cost out to weeks. Second, it tells you less than it seems to: most of that compute goes toward confirming interpolatable behaviour between points you already understand, rather than finding the actual critical condition.

Sample smart, then interpolate with a model, not a lookup table

The alternative is a response-surface based design of experiments: choose a structured, minimal set of CFD runs across the operating-envelope parameters — enough to characterize how temperature responds to each variable and their interactions, not one run per real-world combination — and fit a response surface (or, in the MIDEC method described on the Research page, a dimensionless empirical-corrector formulation) to that sparse dataset. The result is a fast algorithm, not a lookup table: given any altitude/Mach/duty-cycle/bleed combination inside the sampled range, it predicts the LRU's temperature response without a new CFD run.

Two things make this defensible rather than just convenient. First, the DoE sample has to actually span the parameter space intelligently — corners, midpoints and a few interior points, not just the conditions that seem obviously critical, because the true worst case is sometimes not where intuition expects it. Second, every response-surface prediction gets checked against dedicated CFD runs and environmental thermal-chamber test data at a handful of held-out points, so the interpolation's error bars are known rather than assumed.

What it found in practice

On the AEROFLUX programme, this approach is what surfaced the actual critical condition: slow-speed flight at high altitude with 1% engine bleed, rather than the high-Mach, high-bleed condition that might look more thermally aggressive on paper. That's a genuinely useful result — a full brute-force sweep would eventually have found it too, but at several times the compute and schedule cost, and with less insight into why it's critical, since a response-surface fit also shows you the sensitivity of temperature to each input variable, not just the answer at one point.

When this approach is the wrong call

Response-surface methods assume the underlying physics varies smoothly enough across the parameter space to interpolate — they're a poor fit if the system has sharp regime changes (like a flow separation onset or a phase-change threshold) inside the sampled range, since a sparse DoE can miss a real discontinuity between sample points. In those cases, denser sampling around the suspected transition, or falling back to full sweeps in that sub-region, is worth the extra cost.
