Visualizing 2-Way FSI Results Without Losing the Coupling

Two-way fluid-structure interaction produces two solutions that only mean something together: a flow field that deforms a structure, and a deformed structure that changes the flow field back. The visualization mistake I see most often is presenting them separately — a Fluent contour in one image, a Mechanical deformation plot in another — which quietly discards the thing FSI was run to capture in the first place: the coupling itself.

Keep both solution cells linked

In a coupled Fluent + Mechanical system, the practical fix is to bring both solution cells into a single CFD-Post session rather than exporting each one to its own post-processor. With both solutions linked, you can overlay the deformed structural mesh directly on the flow-field contour, at the same solution time step, and show pressure loading and displacement as one consistent picture instead of two that the viewer has to mentally superimpose.

This matters most for anything with a flutter or resonance question attached — a wing, a blade, a slender panel — where the interesting physics is specifically the phase relationship between aerodynamic loading and structural response. A static side-by-side comparison can make a stable, damped response look identical to a growing flutter mode; only the coupled, time-resolved view actually distinguishes them.

When the model gets too large for one post-processor

For larger transient FSI cases — long time histories, fine structural meshes, or several coupled bodies — CFD-Post's linked-solution approach can become unwieldy. EnSight is a reasonable alternative for these cases: it handles multi-domain, multi-solver datasets more comfortably at scale, at the cost of a steeper setup for a one-off analysis. My rule of thumb is to stay in CFD-Post for single-body flutter/aeroelastic studies where the linked-cell workflow is fast to set up, and move to EnSight once the case involves multiple coupled bodies or a transient history long enough that CFD-Post's session files become slow to navigate.

What this looks like in practice

On the UAV aeroelastic flutter work described in Projects, the combined visualization is what actually lets a flutter boundary be read off with confidence — the animation shows the structural mode growing or decaying in step with the aerodynamic pressure field driving it, rather than presenting a displacement number that has to be trusted on its own. That single view tends to answer the stakeholder's real question — "is this going to flutter, and at what condition" — faster than a written table of modal frequencies ever does.
