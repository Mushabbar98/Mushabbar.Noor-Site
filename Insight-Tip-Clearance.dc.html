Why tip clearance punishes small compressors, and axial ones worst of all

When people compare axial and centrifugal compressors, the conversation usually starts with peak efficiency and stage pressure ratio, and on those numbers, a well-designed axial stage often wins at larger scale. But scale that same axial stage down to something you'd fit in a hand-held turbojet, and the comparison inverts, for a reason that rarely makes it onto the textbook chart.

The clearance gap doesn't scale down with the blade

Tip clearance is the small gap between a rotating blade tip and the surrounding casing. It has to exist for the rotor to spin without rubbing. Manufacturing tolerance, thermal growth, and bearing play all set a practical floor on how small that gap can be, and that floor doesn't shrink proportionally as the engine gets smaller. On a large industrial axial compressor, a clearance of a fraction of a millimetre is a rounding error against a blade height of tens of millimetres. Shrink the whole engine down for something in the 50–100 N thrust class, and that same absolute clearance can become a double-digit percentage of the blade span.

That matters because the flow leaking over the tip does no useful work on the rotor. It bypasses the intended flow path, rolls up into a tip-leakage vortex and mixes out downstream, taking pressure rise and efficiency with it. The loss scales roughly with clearance-to-blade-height ratio, so as blade height falls faster than the clearance can be manufactured down, tip losses grow disproportionately. Multi-stage axial designs compound the problem, since every stage pays the same tax.

Why centrifugal sidesteps it

A single-stage centrifugal compressor raises stagnation pressure through a different geometry: flow enters axially and is turned radially outward by a rotating impeller, with the pressure rise coming from both centrifugal effect and diffusion, rather than relying on a long stack of short blades. For a given mass flow and pressure ratio at small scale, a centrifugal impeller can be sized with a much more favourable blade-height-to-clearance ratio than an equivalent axial stage would need, because it doesn't need many short stages to build the same pressure rise.

This is the reasoning that shaped the compressor selection on the small-scale turbojet project on this site: at a 65 mm rotor diameter targeting roughly 80 N thrust, an axial stage would have needed blade heights small enough that tip-clearance losses would have eaten a large share of the efficiency gain axial staging is supposed to offer in the first place. Comparing estimated clearance-to-span ratios for a plausible axial stage against the centrifugal alternative settled the choice before any CFD was run.

Where the trade reverses again

None of this is a permanent rule. It is a statement about where the crossover sits. As rotor diameter grows, manufacturing tolerances become a smaller fraction of blade height, multi-stage axial compression regains its efficiency and pressure-ratio-per-stage advantages, and most large gas turbines are axial for good reason. The practical takeaway for anyone designing at small scale is to run the clearance-ratio number early, before committing to an architecture on the strength of a textbook efficiency curve that assumes a size regime you're not actually in.
